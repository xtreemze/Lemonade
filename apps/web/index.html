Player guide

Run your lemonade stand.

Each day starts with conditions to read and three decisions to make: how many glasses to prepare, how much to advertise, and what price to charge. Commit the day, see what happened, and use the report to plan the next one.

Start playing Read the quick guide

Quick start

One day takes six steps

Read the conditions, make three choices, sell for the day, and use the report as feedback.

1

Check the forecast

Look at the day’s weather and the seller’s confidence before spending money. Conditions can make the same plan perform very differently.

2

Choose glasses

Decide how many cups to prepare. You pay to make them before you know exactly how many people will buy.

3

Choose signs

Advertising can bring in more demand, but every sign costs money. Spend only what the day can reasonably earn back.

4

Set the price

Higher prices earn more per sale but reduce demand. Lower prices attract more buyers but leave less revenue per cup.

5

Sell for the day

Commit your choices and watch Lemonsville react. Your decisions are locked while the day resolves.

6

Read the report

Compare prepared versus sold, revenue versus costs, and the ending balance. Then change tomorrow’s plan based on what actually happened.

Your three choices

Everything you control

The daily operating surface stays deliberately small. Weather and market sentiment are conditions to interpret, not extra controls.

Glasses

How much to make

Prepared glasses set your maximum possible sales for the day. If demand is higher than your inventory, you sell out. If demand is lower, the unused production cost is already spent.

Signs

How hard to advertise

Advertising can increase demand, but it costs cash before the day resolves. Spend only what the rest of the plan can support.

Price

What each cup costs

Price directly trades volume for margin. Raising it reduces potential demand; lowering it can attract more buyers. The best price depends on weather, confidence, inventory, and your available cash.

Cash matters: production and advertising are paid before demand is known. Keep enough operating balance to recover from a weak day.

Conditions

Read the day before you spend

Weather and market sentiment change the context around the same three decisions. Read both before committing cash.

Weather

Better lemonade weather can multiply demand sharply. Poorer weather can make an aggressive inventory or advertising plan expensive.

Strong weatherYou can consider more inventory, more promotion, or testing a higher price—but you still pay every cost up front.
Weak weatherProtect cash. Smaller production and a more cautious spend can reduce waste when fewer customers are likely to buy.

Market sentiment

Sentiment is a demand signal, not a promise of profit. Treat stronger sentiment as more room to test a plan and weaker sentiment as a reason to protect cash.

Stronger sentimentDemand conditions are more favorable, but price, production cost, and inventory still matter.
Weaker sentimentUse smaller commitments and clearer experiments instead of trying to buy your way out of a weak signal.

After closing

How to read the day report

Use the report to compare sales with the cash committed to production and advertising. Revenue alone does not tell you whether the day worked.

  • Sold / preparedShows whether you stocked too little, too much, or roughly enough.
  • RevenueMoney collected from customers before expenses.
  • Production costWhat you spent preparing glasses, including glasses that did not sell.
  • Advertising costWhat you spent on signs before knowing the day’s final demand.
  • Profit or lossThe clearest measure of whether the day’s combination of inventory, promotion, and price worked.
  • Ending assetsYour ability to fund the next day and grow toward a larger stand.

If you sold out

You may have left sales on the table. Next time, consider preparing more—but only if the added production cost still makes sense for similar conditions.

If many cups were left

You probably overproduced for the demand you created. Try less inventory, a lower price, more advertising, or a combination—but change deliberately so you can tell what helped.

Growing the stand

Progression adds responsibility

As the stand grows, the game widens its operating scale and introduces more financial pressure without adding new daily decision variables.

Start small

Early days keep the operating range narrow so the relationship between inventory, advertising, price, and demand stays easy to read.

Scale carefully

More capacity creates more upside and more exposure. Larger production and promotion decisions can make mistakes more expensive.

Manage the business

Later progression adds supplier costs, taxes, banking charges, interest, and credit around the same three daily decisions.

Starter tactics

Learn from each day

Make controlled changes, read the report, and compare similar conditions. The point is to understand the trade-offs, not memorize one fixed setup.

Protect your cash

Production and advertising are paid before the day resolves. Keep enough balance to survive a bad forecast or a poor experiment.

Change one big thing at a time

If you radically change glasses, signs, and price together, the report cannot tell you which change mattered most.

Use sell-outs as information

Selling every glass is not automatically optimal, but repeated sell-outs in similar conditions suggest your inventory ceiling is constraining sales.

Use leftovers as information

Unsold glasses mean you paid for capacity the market did not use. Compare leftovers with price, advertising, weather, and confidence before the next day.

Price is not just profit

A higher price increases revenue per sale while reducing demand. Watch total profit, not just the price tag.

Let conditions change your plan

The same setup should not be repeated automatically every day. Weather and confidence are there to make yesterday’s answer uncertain today.

Behind the game

How demand is calculated

The current model combines advertising, confidence, price, weather, seeded randomness, and the inventory cap. This section is reference material for players who want the mechanics.

The demand equation used by this version

((signs² / log1p(signs) + confidenceRoll × confidence) / priceDollars) × (weatherVariant² + 1)

The result is rounded to a whole number of potential customers, then actual sales are capped by the number of glasses you prepared. In other words: advertising and confidence push demand upward, price pushes it downward, weather can amplify everything, and inventory still sets the final ceiling.

Advertising is nonlinear. The sign term is signs² / log1p(signs), so this revival does not use the original Apple II diminishing-return advertising curve.

Then and now

How the Apple II game differs

The modern game keeps the original spirit—three decisions, weather, uncertainty, and immediate consequences—but it does not copy the Apple II economics exactly.

SystemApple II versionThis Lemonade revival
PriceUsed a 10-cent reference price and a much steeper inverse-square penalty above it.Price enters continuously as an inverse term. There is no special breakpoint where the rules suddenly change.
AdvertisingUsed an exponential response with diminishing returns.Uses the unusual signs² / log1p(signs) term, so advertising behaves very differently and can scale aggressively.
Demand baseStarted from a baseline demand of 30 before price, signs, weather, and events changed it.Builds potential demand from advertising plus confidence, divided by price, then multiplied by weather.
ConfidenceNot the same persistent operating-confidence system used here.Recent business performance changes confidence, and a seeded daily confidence roll feeds demand.
WeatherWeather and special events changed demand through the original BASIC rules.Uses four weather strengths—1×, 2×, 5×, and 10×—with the opening day starting at the strongest state.
RandomnessUsed the period program's random draws.Uses seeded randomness, so the same starting seed and decisions can reproduce a run.
PresentationText, simple color graphics, sound cues, and keyboard interaction on the Apple II.A live 3D Lemonsville, visible customers and signs, accessible reports, charts, progression, and later finance systems around the same three daily choices.

Where the game came from

A classroom business game with a long life

The revival keeps the original game’s short decision-and-feedback loop while rebuilding its presentation for the modern web.

1973

Bob Jamison and MECC

Bob Jamison created the original Lemonade Stand program at the Minnesota Educational Computing Consortium for time-shared computer systems.

1979

Apple II adaptation

Charlie Kellner adapted the program for the Apple II, bringing the business exercise into the early personal-computer classroom.

1980s

Computer-lab learning

Apple II computers became familiar school lab machines, where students learned by trying software, comparing results, and taking turns around shared screens.

Today

Lemonade

The modern revival keeps the small daily decision surface while making the neighborhood, customers, weather, reports, and progression visible around it.
