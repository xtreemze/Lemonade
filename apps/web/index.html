Daily decisions

Three controls, immediate consequences

You commit resources before demand is known. Production sets capacity, signs affect demand, and price trades volume for margin. Weather changes the conditions, but it is not another control.

Glasses prepared

Inventory risk

Every glass you prepare is an upfront cost. Too few creates an inventory ceiling: you cannot sell what you did not make. Too many leaves sunk cost in unsold lemonade. This is the basic inventory-risk problem.

Advertising signs

Advertising return

Signs cost money to deploy, so advertising only helps if the extra demand is worth more than the spend. In the classic-compatible model, signs directly increase demand through the restored historical equation. The planned v4 model instead moves toward saturating reach and bounded advertising fatigue.

Price per glass

Price elasticity

A higher price raises the contribution from each sale but reduces demand. A lower price can move more volume while shrinking unit margin. The decision is a compact form of price elasticity.

Weather

An external demand condition

You do not control the forecast. In the shipping classic-compatible ruleset, weather is a strong multiplier inside potential demand. That teaches a simple point: identical operating decisions can perform very differently when an external condition changes.

Sunny / hotHigher thirst and stronger demand opportunity justify reconsidering both price and capacity.
Cloud / stormDemand can weaken sharply, so committing the same inventory and advertising budget can become expensive.

Confidence

A market-receptivity signal

The project preserves a recognizable 0–5 confidence system. In the current classic-compatible model, a seeded daily confidence roll contributes directly to aggregate demand. In the intended v4 design, confidence becomes a bounded trust/familiarity input rather than a large customer-count generator.

Practical lesson: confidence is not cash. Treat it as information about how receptive the market may be, then still test that signal against price, advertising cost, inventory, and weather.

From timesharing to the Apple II

A small simulation with a long classroom life

Lemonade Stand predates the home-computer boom. Its lineage runs from educational mainframes to the Apple II, where simple graphics, sound, keyboard input, and 5¼-inch disks made the business lesson tangible to students sharing early microcomputer labs.

1973

Bob Jamison and MECC

Bob Jamison created the original program at the Minnesota Educational Computing Consortium for time-shared mainframe systems. The core idea was already there: read the conditions, commit resources, set a price, advertise, and see the business result.

1979

Charlie Kellner adapts it for Apple II

The surviving Applesoft BASIC source credits Jamison’s original and states that Charlie Kellner modified it for the Apple in February 1979. The Apple II version added the immediacy of a personal machine: color graphics, sound, local keyboard input, and disk-based software.

1980s

The computer-lab setting

Apple II systems spread widely through schools. Educational software—including MECC titles—became part of the ritual of scheduled lab time: find a machine, insert a floppy, boot or catalog the disk, type a command, and take turns around one screen.

Today

The same loop, rebuilt for the web

The revival keeps the three-decision loop, adds deterministic simulation and accessible reports, and shows the day resolving in a 3D Lemonsville neighborhood.

Bob Jamison → MECC: original educational business simulation.

Charlie Kellner → Apple II: 1979 adaptation for classroom microcomputers.

MECC → school computer labs: educational software reached shared classroom machines.

Apple II → modern revival: the compact decision loop remains the design reference.

Period context

Microcomputers were becoming school infrastructure

Contemporary education reports describe labs equipped with Apple II/II+ systems, disk drives, monitors, printers, and scheduled student access. The machine was not a personal device in the modern sense; it was often a shared classroom resource.

Why the format worked

Fast feedback made the lesson memorable

The simulation compresses a business cycle into minutes. You form a hypothesis, commit scarce resources, observe a market outcome, and revise. That repeated decision-feedback loop is the educational mechanism that still makes the design useful.
